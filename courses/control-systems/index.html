Introduction to Control Systems

A control system consists of subsystems and processes (also known as plants) which are brought together for the explicit purpose of obtaining a desired output to within a desired performance under a given input. Control systems actually live all around us. For example, the cruise control on a car is a type of control system. Similarly, pacemakers are a type of control system used to help regulate the human heart. Standard drug delivery (think of when you take an aspirin for example) is another type of control system. Thus, it is clear that we have control systems which exist all around us.

There are a couple of major measurements of performance that we generally care about in control systems, one is the transient response, which is how the system responds before reaching a steady state (steady state being generally the asymptotic behavior of the system) and the other is the steady state error, which is the error between the desired steady state (as determined by the input) and the true steady state of the system.

There are two major types of control systems we will examine. One type is called an open-loop control system and the other is called a closed-loop control system. An open-loop control system is one in which the controller recieves the input and then directly applies control to the input, which runs through the process (or plant) which leads to the output. An open-loop system cannot adjust for any kind of disturbance, and thus is sensitive to outside disturbances, and thus to be effective there must be an accurate understanding or model for how the system will respond to the control. An example that we gave above of an open-loop control system is traditional drug delivery. When you take an aspirin, the aspirin directly dissolves in your blood, no matter how much pain you have, the entire thing will dissolve over some period of time. So the aspirin applies a control, but it cannot adjust or adapt to how much pain is in your system.

On the other hand, we have closed-loop control systems. These systems add a loop, typically which includes some kind of sensor, which allows the controller to adjust the controls to ensure the input plus the controlled signal are approaching the desired output state. An example of this is the pacemaker discussed above. The pacemaker sits inside the chambers of the heart and measures the hearts electrical activity, and compares that signal to a reference input (for instance perhaps a rate of 60 beats per minute), if the heartrate drops beneath that reference input, then the pacemakers battery powered generator fires off an electrical charge to help increase the rate.

Introduction to Analysis and Design of Control Systems

Analysis is the process by which a system's performance is determined. Design is the process by which a system's performance is created or changed. It should be noted that a control system is dynamic, that is, it responds to an input by undergoing a transient response before reaching a steady-state response which should resemble the input (or forcing). We should note that the transient response is quite important. For instance, in an elevator, a slow transient response (taking too long to start going) might make the person riding the elevator impatient, but too fast of a transient response might make the person feel queezy from too much acceleration.

As we should now understand from our introduction to differential equations, the steady-state response of the system should resemble the input. Recall for example, in the method of undetermined coefficients, we guessed that the particular solution looked like the input. This is the exact reason, the steady-state should in fact look like the input, at least for linear time invariant systems. We must consider the transient response, since, as already noted, the transient response relates to the comfort or realistic constraints for the system. Too large a transient response could cause biological problems for human riders of an elevator (too large of an acceleration), and too small of a transient response could cause the elevator to take an unbearably long amount of time to operate, as an example. Similarly, the steady state error remains an important consideration, if the elevator stopped halfway in between floors, that would be too large of an error to make it usable.

Perhaps one of the most important things we can analyze in this context will be the stability of the control system. When we are dealing with linear, time invariant differential equations, the total response of the system will be the sum of the natural response and the forced response (by the principle of superposition, ). The natural response relates to how the system dissipates or aquires energy. We will be interested in systems where the natural response approaches a fixed point, (often we will simply assume the fixed point is at \(0\) for simplicity and any system can be translated to make this true). Such a system is stable. For the purposes of this course we will not consider the system stable (or call it instable) if it grows or oscillates (growth is always considered unstable, but in some control theoretic contexts oscillations can be considered stable if one approaches a limit cycle as time goes to \(\infty\) rather than having growing oscillations). In a control system, oscillations can be devastating, take for example the crash of an F-22 prototype jet which happened due to something called "Pilot Induced Oscillations" (see the following link: PIO ). In this example, there was a small time delay between the control signal and the output. The delay was enough that the pilot thought that they had not pushed on the control stick hard enough, so the pilot tried to push harder, causing a more violent oscillatory response, which caused the pilot to try to overcorrect in the opposite direction leading to larger oscillations and eventually to the crash.

Other Considerations for Control Systems

As already noted above, when designing a control system, one must account for the transient response, the steady-state error and the stability of the system. However, there are a number of other practical things that must be accounted for as well, for example hardware selection, one must account for the correct pieces and measurement devices (for closed loop control) to create the control system. Another consideration is the financial cost of creating the system. If the system becomes overly expensive, there may not be any customers to purchase the control system, so there often needs to be trade offs to account for cost. Another consideration is the robustness of the design (this is often called structural stability in dynamical systems) , if your design system is sensitive to small parameter changes then it is not very robust and will likely face challenges once it moves into the real world.

Electrical Network Transfer Functions Part 1

We should begin by explaining some modeling elements of linear (remember for transfer functions we are assuming linear time invariant) electrical systems. The first thing we will discuss is Ohm's law, which is a law for linear resistors, and it states that, $$e_R = iR \implies R = \frac{e_R}{i},$$ where \(i\) is the current (which is the same as \(\frac{dq}{dt}\) where \(q\) is the charge), \(e_R\) is the voltage across the resistor and \(R\) is the resistance of the resistor. So a resistor represents one common element of an electrical network, so does a capacitor. The capacitance is given by, $$C = \frac{q}{e_C},$$ where \(C\) is the capacitance, \(e_C\) is the voltage across the (linear) capacitor. We can rewrite this in terms of the current, recalling that \(i = \frac{dq}{dt}\) and rewriting, \(e_C = \frac{q}{C}\), if I differentiate this equation, $$\frac{de_C}{dt} = \frac{1}{C}\frac{dq}{dt} \implies C\frac{de_C}{dt} = i.$$ Thus, if I want to write the voltage out in terms of \(i\) (which will be useful thanks to Kirchoffs laws), I need to integrate both sides to find $$e_C(t) = \frac{1}{C}\int \limits_{0}^t i dt + e_C(0).$$ Finally, we will discuss inductance elements. The inductance can be written as, $$L = \frac{e_L}{\frac{di}{dt}} \implies e_L = L\frac{di}{dt},$$ where \(e_C\) is the voltage across the (linear) inductor, and \(L\) is the inductance. If we so need, we may write things in terms of the current accross the capacitor, $$i_L(t) = \frac{1}{L}\int \limits_{0}^{t}e_L dt + i_L(0).$$

In series circuits (where elements are all in a line connected together, and the path of the current must leave one element and directly enter the next), we may write the voltages as follows, $$e = \sum \limits_k e_k,$$ where \(e_k\) is the \(k\)th voltage element. Frequently we will be interested in how elements either in series, in parallel, or a mix of the 2 behave. In series, resistors based on the sum rule above follow the same sum rule, this is easy to see, since \(e = iR\), thus, $$e = \sum \limits_k i_kR_k.$$ Thus in series, $$R_{eq} = R_1 + ...+ R_n,$$ where \(R_{eq}\) is the equivalent resistance, if there are \(n\) series connected resistors. You will find, by the same logic, that inductance elements obey this same rule. However, due to the relationship between voltage and capacitance (namely voltage on one side means \(\frac{1}{C}\) on the other side of the equation) we get that in series $$\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{1}{C_1}+ ...+\frac{1}{C_n}.$$

In addition, to the element rules noted above, we also will have two variations of something called Kirchhoff's laws, one being the loop law for voltages, the other being the node law for currents. The node law for currents indicates that, $$\sum \limits_{k} i_k = 0,$$ that is the sum of all of the currents across a particular node should be zero. We will often use the convention that the currents entering a node are positive and those leaving a node are negative, though the reverse convention is equally usable (since they sum to zero, we may flip the signs across the sum). A similar rule for voltages arises, though this one states that the sum of the voltages around any loop are equal to zero. We will use the convention that the flow from the negative to the positive terminal of a voltage source will result in an increase in voltage, and a drop in voltage occurs in the opposite direction. Additionally, if we are moving in the same direction as the current flow, then a voltage decrease happens through a resistor, while going the opposite direction of the current flow through a resistor will lead to an increase in voltage.
